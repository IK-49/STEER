STEER · CDC @ UNC datathon presentation notes
================================================

Three-minute demo. The framing is deliberate: this is a descriptive school
community-profile explorer, not a funding, intervention, or school-quality
decision system.

THREE-MINUTE SCRIPT
-------------------

0:00–0:35 | The question

“A single composite score can summarize a lot of information, but it cannot
show every difference among domains. STEER lets a user inspect a selected
school’s five ODIS community-stress scores and find records that are nearby in
the chosen standardized feature space.”

“Our source’s federal school identifier field is unreliable for this use, so
we do not load it. We identify a record by school name, city, state, and
district, falling back to county if district is missing. Exact duplicate
profiles collapse; conflicting profiles with the same name/location key stop
with an error instead of being guessed.”

0:35–1:35 | Live demo

“I can type a school name and see the matching suggestions update immediately.
I’ll select Northeast Regional, choose domains, and set the geographic scope.
STEER standardizes each selected score over the candidate pool and ranks the
K smallest Euclidean distances. The table shows the raw distance and the
largest per-domain contribution to that distance.”

“If a candidate is missing a selected score, it is left out of that search
and counted. We do not impute. The state filter changes the candidate pool,
so it can change both the standardization and the nearest records.”

“The other tabs preserve Izad’s profile-comparison and domain-spread ideas.
One compares nearby community profiles with higher Education scores; that is
an observed association, not evidence of effective practice or causation.
The leaderboard ranks spread across selected domain z-scores. It does not
rank need or show that a composite score is biased.”

“The what-if control changes a score mathematically to show how the spread
metric responds. It does not predict what a real intervention would do. The
program reference is a starting point for independent research, not a grant
recommendation or eligibility decision.”

1:35–2:20 | Method and limits

“ODIS is the Open Data Index for Schools. Its domain scores describe
community-stress indicators synthesized to School Attendance Boundaries.
They are not school performance scores. The source report describes measures
from different source years, geographies, and underlying datasets.”

“The legacy CMI is the standard deviation of a selected school’s domain
z-scores relative to this search’s candidate pool. We call it domain-profile
spread because that is what it calculates. The result changes with the chosen
domains and candidate pool; it is not an established measure of harm, equity,
or formula distortion.”

“Distance is not a percentage or probability. Standardization puts features
on comparable numeric scales for this calculation; it does not make their
policy meanings interchangeable.”

2:20–3:00 | Close

“STEER is a transparent exploratory prototype that keeps the calculation
visible. A responsible next step is to ask data owners and school-community
stakeholders whether the identity rules, peer comparisons, and missing-data
policy are useful for a real decision. We would validate that before making
recommendations or building collaboration workflows.”

LIKELY JUDGE QUESTIONS
----------------------

Q: Why not repair or use NCESSCH?
A: We could not reliably recover that field from this source. This version
   deliberately does not read it. Its key uses source name and location text.
   That avoids the damaged field, but it cannot guarantee a globally unique
   identity. Conflicting profiles on the same key stop loading; a future
   production system needs a trusted identity crosswalk.

Q: How is a school with the same name distinguished?
A: By city, state, and district, with county as a fallback when district is
   absent. If the remaining key maps to different profiles, we report the
   ambiguity and do not choose one.

Q: What does the nearest school mean?
A: It has a small Euclidean distance after standardizing the selected domain
   scores across the candidate set. This only means the measured values are
   close under these features and this scope. It says nothing about causes,
   outcomes, or transferable programs.

Q: Why does changing state change distances?
A: The candidate pool is filtered first, and StandardScaler is fit on that
   pool. Different pools have different means and standard deviations. That
   makes the comparison local to the chosen search context.

Q: What is the CMI / domain-profile spread?
A: The population standard deviation of the target’s selected domain
   z-scores, relative to the candidate pool. We retained the calculation as
   an exploratory summary but do not treat it as a validated equity metric.

Q: Can I use the score adjustment to estimate the impact of a policy?
A: No. It changes a score input and recomputes the descriptive metric. It has
   no treatment model, counterfactual, or causal identification strategy.

Q: Why show program names?
A: The legacy feature links the highest relative domain to a possible area
   for further research. It is not an eligibility check or funding
   recommendation. Program rules and school-level facts are outside this
   dataset and must be independently verified.

Q: What does the positive-deviance tab show?
A: It looks for records nearby on Economic, Health, Housing, and Crime scores
   that have higher Education scores. It does not observe practices, explain
   the difference, or prove that one school is a mentor.

CLAIMS TO AVOID
--------------

- Do not say the nearest records are validated twins or causal comparators.
- Do not call profile spread a proven measure of inequity, crisis, or masking.
- Do not say the what-if solver predicts an intervention or policy effect.
- Do not say STEER establishes grant fit, eligibility, or allocation.
- Do not describe ODIS neighborhood measures as school performance scores.
- Do not call a higher Education score evidence of best practice.
- Do not present distance as a calibrated percentage or probability.
